Scope & themes

IMHWS-1 covers marine heatwaves in the widest sense: how we define and detect them, what drives them, whether we can predict them, what they do to marine life, what that costs people, and what anyone can do about it. If your work concerns marine heatwaves, it is in scope.

There is no separate call for sessions

Most conferences ask you to propose a session, then ask everyone else to submit into the sessions that got accepted. That decides the shape of the meeting before anyone knows what the community actually wants to present, and it rewards whoever happened to be organised in the right month. We are doing it the other way round. Submit the abstract you want to give. Once submissions close we will group them into sessions, and the programme will reflect what was sent rather than what we guessed a year earlier.

The kinds of work this covers

These are not sessions and you do not need to pick one. They are here so you can see the breadth of what belongs at this meeting, and to make the point that a marine heatwave symposium is not only a physical oceanography meeting.

  • 1

    Definition, detection & data

    The unglamorous questions underneath everything else, several of which the field still disagrees about.

    • Baselines: fixed, shifting or detrended, and what a climatology means in an ocean that is warming underneath it
    • Whether intensity categories actually map onto ecological consequence
    • Subsurface events, and detecting them with sparse observations
    • Compound events: heat with deoxygenation, acidification, freshwater or light stress
    • Disagreement between satellite, in situ and reanalysis products
    • Marine cold spells, as the informative counterpoint
  • 2

    Physical drivers & mechanisms

    Why a particular event happened, where the heat came from, and what ended it.

    • Partitioning air–sea flux, advection and mixing
    • Boundary currents, eddies and the role of ocean dynamics
    • Teleconnections with ENSO, the IOD, SAM and atmospheric blocking
    • Stratification, mixed-layer depth and event persistence
    • Vertical structure and the relationship between surface and depth
    • Termination: why some events collapse abruptly and others decay
  • 3

    Prediction, projection & attribution

    Forecasting that gets tested, and projections that are honest about what they can and cannot resolve.

    • Subseasonal to seasonal forecast skill, and where predictability runs out
    • Early warning systems that someone actually uses to make a decision
    • Model projections, emergence times and the limits of coarse resolution
    • Downscaling for shelf seas and coastal systems
    • Attribution of individual events
    • Forecasts that failed, and a diagnosis of why
  • 4

    Biological & ecological responses

    What heat does to organisms, populations and ecosystems, measured rather than assumed.

    • Thermal physiology, tolerance limits and sublethal effects
    • Mass mortality: kelp, coral, seagrass, invertebrates, seabirds, mammals
    • Range shifts, tropicalisation and community reorganisation
    • Recovery, resistance, hysteresis and regime shifts
    • Cumulative and sequential events, and the ecology of no recovery time
    • Acclimation, adaptive capacity and evolutionary responses
    • Food-web and trophic consequences
  • 5

    People, economies & societies

    The half of this problem that physical and ecological work alone cannot reach.

    • Fisheries and aquaculture losses, closures and quota consequences
    • Food security and small-scale fisheries livelihoods
    • Indigenous and local knowledge, and sea country relationships
    • Public perception, media framing and why a marine heatwave is harder to see than a terrestrial one
    • Insurance, markets and supply chains
    • Governance, law and who carries the cost
  • 6

    Management, mitigation & adaptation

    What can actually be done, and evidence about whether it worked.

    • Turning a forecast into a decision someone is willing to make
    • Dynamic ocean management and real-time response
    • Protected area design when thermal regimes move
    • Restoration, refugia and assisted adaptation
    • Decision-making under deep uncertainty
    • Interventions that have been implemented, and how they performed

Particularly welcome

A few kinds of contribution that conferences systematically under-represent, and that we would like to see more of at this one.

Genuinely interdisciplinary work

Studies that connect physical drivers to biological outcomes to human consequences, rather than stopping at the boundary of one discipline and speculating about the next. This is the hardest kind of work to publish and the easiest kind to present.

Interactions and compound events

Heat rarely arrives alone. Work on marine heatwaves together with acidification, deoxygenation, freshwater input, storms or disease, and on the cases where the combination does something none of the stressors does by itself.

Societal impacts, management and adaptation

Consequences for fisheries, aquaculture, tourism and coastal communities, and what was actually done about them: management responses, industry adaptation, mitigation, and honest accounts of what worked and what did not.

Under-observed regions and phenomena

Much of the tropical Atlantic, the Indian Ocean rim, South East Asia, South America and the polar oceans are thinly represented in this literature, as are subsurface and benthic events anywhere. If you work there, your first characterisation is new work.

Long records and historical events

Palaeo and historical reconstructions, long monitoring series, and re-analysis of events that predate the satellite era or the term itself.

Methods, data and tools

Observing systems, detection software, open datasets, reproducible workflows. Infrastructure work is easy to overlook and much of the field depends on it.

Negative and null results

Events that caused less damage than expected, systems that proved more resilient than predicted, methods that did not work. These are as informative as the headline mortality events and far less often presented.

What counts as a marine heatwave here

The working definition is the qualitative one the field already shares: a prolonged, discrete, anomalously warm water event, describable by its duration, intensity, rate of onset and decline, and spatial extent. That is the floor. How you then quantify it — which baseline, which percentile, which minimum duration — is a question this symposium is interested in rather than one it has settled in advance.

Beyond that, deliberately broadly. Surface or subsurface. Open ocean, shelf, coastal, estuarine or polar. Defined against a fixed baseline, a shifting one, or by a definition you will argue for from the podium. Days to seasons. We would also rather include work on marine cold spells and on compound extremes than draw a boundary that excludes the comparisons that make heat events interpretable.

If you are unsure whether something fits, it almost certainly does. Ask us at IMHWS2027@gmail.com rather than self-rejecting.

Why we are meeting in person

IMHWS-1 is an in-person meeting. There is no virtual or hybrid attendance option. We want to be straightforward about that, and about the fact that a symposium on ocean climate extremes is asking several hundred people to fly.

Air travel is a real contribution to the problem this meeting exists to study, and we are not going to pretend otherwise. The question is whether what this particular meeting is for can be achieved another way. We think it cannot. The purpose of IMHWS-1 is to put physical oceanographers, ecologists, modellers, social scientists and the people who manage fisheries and reefs in one room for four days — communities that publish in different journals, attend different conferences and largely do not meet. Forming new collaborations across disciplinary boundaries is precisely the thing online formats do worst. Existing collaborations survive on video calls; new ones, between people who do not yet know they need each other, overwhelmingly do not start on them.

Holding it in Perth is deliberate for related reasons. Western Australia has experienced some of the most consequential marine heatwaves yet recorded, and the Indian Ocean and the southern hemisphere are under-represented in this literature in a way that has shaped what the field thinks it knows. Meeting here brings researchers to the systems rather than talking about them from elsewhere, and makes it substantially easier for colleagues across the Indian Ocean rim, South East Asia and Australasia to attend than a European or North American venue would.

Since we are asking for the flights, we intend to make them count:

  • A single four-day meeting rather than a series of shorter ones, so one trip does the work of several.
  • Workshops and field activities scheduled either side of the main programme, so people travelling a long way can do more than one thing.
  • Talks recorded and made openly available afterwards, so the content reaches people who could not travel even though the room cannot.
  • No conference bags, no printed programme, no branded merchandise.

We would rather state the trade-off plainly than leave it unaddressed. If you think we have the balance wrong, we would like to hear it.
